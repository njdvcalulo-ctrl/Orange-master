# Chapter 2: Dawn of Healing

Morning after row seven, Orange woke sore in a good way.

His shoulders pulled when he reached for his shirt. His palms showed thin new skin where the blisters had been. He pressed one thumb across them and felt only a dull tightness, already loosening.

*Seven for the week,* he thought. *Today we rest.*

He carried water to Marie's garden beds first. A horned rabbit watched from the grass and decided not to run.

"Smart," Orange told it. "Yesterday you paid rent. Today you rest too."

He left his hoe by the gate. Work could wait. Today was the race.

Amy found him before he found her.

"I am fastest," Amy announced, hands on hips, by the west route. "Yesterday's frog was big. Today I am bigger fast."

"You were not fast yesterday," Leo said. He carried a basket of barn feed like it weighed nothing. It weighed a lot. "You screamed at a frog."

"It was a BIG frog," Amy said. "And today I race Orange and Lyra and you. Loser carries water."

Orange grinned. "Short race. Field edge to the old marker stone. We stop at the stone."

Lyra nodded from the shade, bow over her shoulder. She had been watching the forest edge all morning. Quiet, as always.

"To the stone," Lyra agreed. "Not past."

They ran.

Orange loved running almost as much as hoeing. Feet slapping dirt. Breath burning clean. Amy ahead, laughing. Leo thundering behind, basket banging. Lyra light and silent between them.

"I'm winning!" Amy shouted.

She reached the marker stone first. She slapped it. Then she kept running.

"Amy!" Orange called. "Stone! Stop at stone!"

But the game had changed. The field had ended. The grass past the stone stood taller, wilder, uncut. The air smelled damp and green. Forest edge.

Amy slowed. Leo slowed. Lyra stopped dead.

"Back," Lyra said softly. "This is the border."

Before them, in a hollow between roots, something round shone in the morning light.

A slime.

It was small, no bigger than a water bucket, green and trembling. Pure sphere. No arms. No face. Just wet shine holding its shape. It wobbled once where it sat, guarding its spot.

"Whoa," Leo breathed. "Real one."

"It is cute," Amy whispered. She took one step closer. "Hey little—"

The slime pulsed.

It spat. Not far. Not fast. A splash of clear wet caught Leo's forearm as he threw it up over his face. Another drop kissed Amy's shin.

Amy screamed. Leo grunted and stumbled back.

"It burns!" Amy cried, dropping to the dirt. Her leg showed an angry red patch, like a bad sunburn. Leo's arm matched it, red and tight and stinging.

Lyra was already moving. She grabbed Amy's arm. Orange grabbed Leo's belt.

"Run!" Orange shouted.

They ran.

The good thing, the only good thing, was the slime did not chase. It wobbled back into its hollow, rippling, and settled. Guarding its spot. Not hunting. Just defending.

At the marker stone they stopped. Safe ground. Familiar dirt.

Amy cried now, loud and honest. Leo held his arm tight to his chest, jaw clenched, trying not to cry.

Orange looked at Leo's arm first. "Leo?"

"I was protecting my face," Leo muttered, turning half away. "I am fine. Check Amy."

"You are not fine," Lyra said. "Sit. Both."

Orange knelt beside Amy. His hands shook.

Amy could not stand right. Her leg trembled when she tried. Leo's burn throbbed under his fingers.

Orange had hoed all week. Seven rows, finished yesterday. He had carried water this morning. He had felt that small warmth loosen his blisters yesterday.

This was different. This was not his own tired muscle. This was his friends hurting in front of him, scared of the sting and the scolding waiting at home.

They had to rest. Then they had to go home. Nobody out here knew who could help.

The warmth heals me, Orange thought. Will it help others too? I should try.

Amy grabbed his sleeve. "Orange. It hurts."

"Can I look?" Orange asked. "Just — hold still."

He put his hands just above her burn. He did not touch it. He was afraid to make it worse.

He closed his eyes for half a breath.

*Thank you for this morning. Please, help me help them. I do not know how. Use me if You can.*

The warmth under his skin answered.

Not hot. Warm. Like soup steam on a cold face. It moved down his arms to his palms. His hands tingled.

Amy gasped. The red on her shin softened at the edges. The tight shine eased. Not gone. Not perfect. But better, enough that she stopped crying and stared.

"Warm," she whispered, staring. "Orange — your hands are warm. How—"

Orange stared at his own hands. He had never done that before. Not to blisters. Not to anyone. His fingers trembled.

"I don't know," he whispered. "I just didn't want you hurting."

Leo's eyes went round. "Whoa. That is so cool. Do me too!"

Orange swallowed, sweating now. He moved to Leo, afraid to make it worse. Same half-breath. Same quiet ask. Same warmth, thinner this time, slower. Leo breathed out hard through his nose.

"Still stings," Leo said honestly, eyes wide. "But I can move it. You did that?"

Orange sat back in the dirt. His arms felt heavy, like after ten rows instead of seven. His chest rose fast. The warmth hid again, tired.

He had not fixed them. He had helped them keep going.

Lyra watched his face, not his hands.

"You asked someone," she said quietly. "I never saw that before."

Orange nodded, too tired to explain.

Lyra squeezed his shoulder once. "Good. Then we walk back together. Thank you for sharing it. It mattered."

The walk back was slow. Amy leaned on Lyra. Leo carried his basket one-handed. Orange carried Amy's stick and his own shaking legs.

Ylila saw them coming.

Marie was first, basket dropping from her hip. Amy's father shouted from the lake path. Leo's mother ran from the smithy with a wet cloth already in her hand.

Then everyone talked at once.

"Where were you!"

"Past the stone? Past the marker?"

"Show me. Let me see."

"Yes — past the stone," Amy said quickly. "Sorry. We saw a slime and it spit on us. Look!"

She held out her shin. Leo held out his arm. Clean red patches, already easing. Worse before, they said.

"It was bigger earlier," Leo said. "But Orange — he did something. Like a heal, from the stories. His hands went warm."

The burns were cleaned with cool lake water at the well. No blood. No torn skin. Marie's hands were steady but her eyes were wet.

The adults around the well exchanged looks. That was new. That would be talked about.

"You could have been worse hurt," she said to all four, which meant mostly to Orange.

John knelt to Orange's height. He looked at the burns. He looked at Orange's pale face.

"Thank you for doing what you could," John said. "Tell me about it at home, okay?"

"I just—" Orange started. "It was warm and I—"

John ruffled his hair gently. "At home."

The village chief arrived last. Chief Edward, big and slow, broad shoulders under a chief's coat, beard neat, voice warm and strong. He looked at the burns. He looked at the marker stone far behind them. Then he turned to the field watchers, calm and plain.

"Rest," Edward told the kids. "Eat. Then sleep. Questions can wait until the sting is gone."

"No panic," Edward said to the watchers. "Two of you to the hollow. Stay sharp. Report anything strange, then back."

Behind him, two field watchers were already walking toward the border with spears, not to kill, just to check the hollow and make sure the slime had stayed in its spot.

It had.

Supper at the Oathkeeper house was quiet.

Steamed rice, lake fish, hot soup, mango juice. Marie set the bowls down and did not tell anyone to laugh. Orange ate with both hands around the bowl, letting the steam warm his face. His arms still felt heavy. His palms still tingled.

Amy had gone home to broth, a scolding, and a hug. Leo was with his parents, pretending he did not need rest. Lyra had slipped home silent.

"Does it still hurt them?" Orange asked into his soup.

"Less now," Marie said. "Because you stayed with them."

Orange frowned. "I did not fix it all the way."

"No," John said. "You did what you could. That is the first job."

Orange closed his eyes for half a breath before his second bowl.

*Thank you that we got back. Thank you for the warmth. Teach me how to use it right.*

That night Orange dreamed of a green sprout, no taller than his thumb, leaning toward him.

Nothing burned. No words. Just the sprout, and dark soft soil holding it up.
