# Chapter 1: Row Seven

The hoe came down. The soil gave way. The shock ran up Orange's arms like a bell rung too hard.

"Six," Orange panted. "Row six before breakfast."

He spat on his palms, rubbed them together, and swung again. The Oathkeeper home plot stretched ahead of him in straight dark rows, each no longer than twenty paces, wet from last night's rain. Behind him, the Great Mango tree stood over Ylila's sleeping houses, its leaves rustling like it was laughing at him.

"Almost," Orange told the field. "Watch me."

The blade bit. Row six fell in behind him. He straightened up, grinning, and looked at his hands.

Two bright blisters stood on his palms. Honest farm blisters. Every farm kid earned them.

Orange frowned. He rubbed a thumb over the edge of one. The sting was already dulling. The tight skin softening, like water smoothing off leaves. Not instantly. Not perfectly. But faster than it should have.

Too fast.

Last week's blisters had stung all morning. These ones were barely warm.

"...Huh," Orange said. "Faster than last week."

He clenched his fist once, testing. No real pain. Just strength, and underneath the tiredness, a small warmth he could not steer. There, then hiding again.

It had taken him years to notice it. Longer still to understand other kids didn't have it.

Leo rested when he was tired. Amy complained loudly when she was hurt. Loudly, and often, and to everyone. Orange just... kept going.

"What are you?" he whispered to the warmth.

No answer. Just the wind through the mango leaves.

A horned rabbit bolted out of Marie's kamatis beds with a leafy prize in its mouth. It skidded to a stop, saw Orange, and froze.

Orange froze.

They stared at each other.

"Don't," Orange warned. "That's my mom's soup you're stealing."

The rabbit twitched its nose. Then, with great dignity, it turned around and hopped off in the opposite direction. With the leaf. At a walk. Like paying rent was for other rabbits.

"Hey!" Orange laughed, chasing it three steps before remembering his hoe and his rows. "You come back here and — ah, forget it. You're too slow to be a real thief anyway."

The rabbit vanished into the grass. Correct decision.

---

"Don't push yourself too hard."

His mother's voice drifted from the field edge, soft but firm.

Marie Oathkeeper stood there with a basket on her hip, watching him with that look mothers have — half worry, half pride, fully seeing through you.

She had said those same words every morning for as long as he could remember.

"I'm not," Orange said quickly, hiding his blistered palms behind his back like a thief hiding a kamatis leaf.

Marie raised one eyebrow. "Orange."

"Okay, a little," he admitted, grinning. "But it's a good kind of hard!"

She sighed, but she was smiling. "Breakfast is ready. Wash your hands. And feet. And is — is that mud in your hair already?"

"Maybe."

---

Breakfast was loud.

Marie's carrot soup steamed up to the roof beams, sweet and golden. Orange closed his eyes for half a breath before picking up his spoon.

*Thank you for this morning. For the field. For Mom's soup.*

Short. Sincere. Not for show. Then he dug in.

John Oathkeeper ate standing up, already half out the door with his own hoe over his shoulder. He ruffled Orange's hair hard enough to sting.

"Farm first," John said. "Chatter later."

"He dreams while hoeing now," Marie said fondly, setting down his bowl. "My boy, dreaming with his mouth full."

"Hey!" Orange protested through a mouthful of carrot. "Big dreams, Mom. They need feeding too."

John laughed — a short, warm bark. "Six rows?"

"Six," Orange said, puffing up. "Before breakfast."

"Six and a half," Marie corrected, setting down mango juice. "I counted. And you chased a rabbit instead of finishing row seven."

Orange stared at her. "How did you — were you watching the whole time?"

"I'm your mother," Marie said, like that explained everything. "I see the rows. I see the rabbit. I see the blisters you're hiding. You push like you think the pain will leave on its own."

Orange looked down at his bowl. "...The rabbit started it."

"Finish your soup," Marie said, "and then you can go declare war on the rest of the field."

---

Outside, the village was fully awake now — and already loud.

Amy's voice carried clear across the lane, arguing with her father about whose catch had been bigger yesterday. "Mine!" "Yours was a frog, Amy." "It was a BIG frog!"

Leo hauled a basket of barn feed past the smithy, knees wobbling, pretending it wasn't heavy. It was very heavy. His face said otherwise. His arms said the truth.

Lyra slipped between houses without a sound, bow over her shoulder. She paused beside Orange's gate.

"Race tomorrow?" Orange asked. "To the marker stone?"

Lyra glanced toward the field edge. "Let's stop at the stone."

"Deal," Orange grinned. "Loser hauls water."

Lyra nodded once and vanished toward the trees, silent as ever.

Same morning. Same village. Same people Orange had known his whole life.

And yet, hoeing his rows, Orange had felt something tugging at him. Not the warmth. Something wider. As if the field wasn't the edge of his world anymore.

As if it was the beginning of it.

---

The afternoon belonged to row seven.

The sun climbed high and the soil dried and hardened, and row seven fought him for every handspan. His arms burned. His back complained. Twice he stopped to drink from the ladle by the field bucket, and twice he went back in.

"Come on," he muttered at the hoe. "You and me. We finish."

He finished. Seven full rows, straight as combed hair, standing over them with dirt on his face and his chest heaving and that small warmth moving quietly under his tiredness, loosening what was tight.

"Eight tomorrow," Orange announced to the rows. "Then the race. Then we'll see."

The mango leaves rustled. It sounded like applause, if you were the kind of boy who heard such things.

Orange was exactly that kind of boy.

---

Supper was quiet. Soup again, bread, mango juice. Orange ate with both hands around the bowl and told his parents about row seven, which he had finished, and row eight, which he had started and would absolutely finish tomorrow.

"Sleep," Marie said, ruffling his hair the gentle way. "The field will still be there."

It was. In his dreams, it was burning.

---

Fire.

Orange knew this field. The rows ran straight as combed hair — until the flames ate them.

The fire was already out. Black stalks. Cracked soil. Smoke that smelled like burnt rice.

Under the ash, something warm pulsed — not searing, warm.

He should have run.

Instead he knelt — scary, but familiar, so he stayed. The same warmth that lived under his own skin, only bigger. Older.

A green sprout pushed through the black, no taller than his thumb.

It leaned toward him. Like it knew him.

A voice, or the memory of one:

*"What you refuse to tend will be lost."*

---

Orange sat up in the dark, heart thumping.

His room. His bed. The mango leaves rustling outside his window. No fire. No ash.

But the words were still there, hanging behind his eyes like smoke that wouldn't clear.

*What you refuse to tend will be lost.*

Orange pressed a hand to his chest. Warm. Faint, like embers under ash. Then gone — or hiding.

"Okay," he whispered to the dark. "Okay."

Tomorrow: row eight. Then the race to the stone.

He lay back down and stared at the roof beams, turning the words over like a smooth stone from the river. What you refuse to tend will be lost. His rows. His friends. The whole small village breathing around him in the dark.

He slept at last, and the field waited.
