# Chapter 1: The Boy Who Didn't Break

Fire.

Orange knew this field. The rows ran straight as combed hair — until the flames ate them.

The fire was already out. Black stalks. Cracked soil. Smoke that smelled like burnt rice.

He should have run.

Instead he knelt — scary, but familiar, so he stayed.

Under the ash, something warm pulsed — not searing, warm — the same warmth that lived under his own skin, only bigger. Older.

A green sprout pushed through the black, no taller than his thumb.

It leaned toward him. Like it knew him.

A voice, or the memory of one:

"What you refuse to tend will be lost."

---

Orange reached out —

and woke.

The roof beams of his own room stared back at him. Cool morning air slipped through the window cracks. Somewhere outside, a rooster crowed like it was personally offended by sleep.

Orange sat up, heart thumping.

"That dream again," he whispered.

Not every night. Just sometimes. Sometimes it was only smoke and heat. Sometimes there was a voice. This time there had been a sprout, leaning toward him like a friend.

He pressed a hand to his chest. Warm. Faint, like embers under ash. Then gone — or hiding.

"Still there," he muttered, and grinned despite himself. "Okay. Good morning to you too."

He dressed fast — plain shirt, farm pants, bare feet for the field — and slipped out before the sun had even bothered to climb over the Great Mango.

Ylila was still half-asleep. Mist hung low over the rice paddies. The lake glinted gray-pink at the edge of the village. The Great Mango stood in the center like a sleepy giant, leaves rustling.

Orange loved this hour. It felt like the whole village belonged to whoever woke up first.

Which was usually him. Which was exactly why he had a plan.

"Today," he announced to absolutely no one, grabbing his worn wooden hoe from beside the door, "I beat yesterday."

Yesterday he had hoed six rows before breakfast. Today: seven. Minimum. Eight if his arms didn't complain too loud.

He marched to the Oathkeeper field at the edge of the ring streets, where the soil was dark and heavy from last night's rain.

He spat on his palms, rubbed them together, and swung.

Once. Twice. Again.

The blade bit in. The shock ran up his arms. The soil was stubborn and unyielding.

Just the way he liked it.

By row three, his breath was coming short. By row four, his palms were burning.

"Ha!" Orange panted. "Is that all you've got?"

The field, wisely, said nothing.

By row five, a small audience had arrived.

A horned rabbit — fat, gray, with one floppy horn — bolted out of Marie's kamatis beds with a leafy prize in its mouth. It skidded to a stop, saw Orange, and froze.

Orange froze.

They stared at each other.

"Don't," Orange warned. "That's my mom's soup you're stealing."

The rabbit twitched its nose. Then, with great dignity, it turned around and hopped off in the opposite direction. With the leaf. At a walk. Like paying rent was for other rabbits.

"Hey!" Orange laughed, chasing it three steps before remembering his hoe and his seven rows. "You come back here and — ah, forget it. You're too slow to be a real thief anyway."

The rabbit vanished into the grass. Correct decision.

Orange wiped his forehead and looked down at his hands.

Two bright blisters stood out on his palms. Honest work blisters. Every farm kid earned them.

"Ow," he said honestly.

He sat back on his heels and frowned at them. Last week's blisters had stung all morning. These ones —

He rubbed a thumb over the edge. The sting was already dulling. The tight skin softening, like water smoothing off leaves. Not instantly. Not perfectly. But faster than it should have.

Too fast.

"...Huh," Orange said. "Faster than last week."

He clenched his fist once, testing. No real pain. Just strength, and underneath the tiredness, that faint warmth he could not steer — there, then hiding again.

It had taken him years to notice it. Longer still to understand other kids didn't have it.

Leo rested when he was tired. Amy complained loudly when she was hurt — loudly, and often, and to everyone. Orange... didn't stay tired for long.

"What are you?" he whispered to the warmth.

No answer. Just the wind through the mango leaves.

"Don't push yourself too hard."

His mother's voice drifted from the field edge, soft but firm.

Marie Oathkeeper stood there with a basket on her hip, watching him with that look mothers have — half worry, half pride, fully seeing through you.

She had said those same words every morning for as long as he could remember.

"I'm not," Orange said quickly, hiding his blistered palms behind his back like a thief hiding a kamatis leaf.

Marie raised one eyebrow. "Orange."

"Okay, a little," he admitted, grinning. "But it's a good kind of hard!"

She sighed, but she was smiling. "Breakfast is ready. Wash your hands. And feet. And — is that mud in your hair already?"

"Maybe."

---

Breakfast was loud.

Marie's carrot soup steamed up to the roof beams, sweet and golden. Orange closed his eyes for half a breath before picking up his spoon.

*Thank you for this morning. For the field. For Mom's soup.*

Short. Sincere. Not for show. No one teased him for it. That half-breath was just Orange.

Then he dug in.

John Oathkeeper ate standing up, already half out the door with his own hoe over his shoulder. He ruffled Orange's hair hard enough to sting.

"Farm first," John said. "Philosophy later."

"He dreams while hoeing now," Marie told the soup pot fondly. "My boy, the turnip philosopher."

"Hey!" Orange protested through a mouthful of carrot. "It was a mango philosopher at least. Turnips don't dream that big."

John laughed — a short, warm bark. "Seven rows?"

"Seven," Orange said, puffing up. "Before breakfast."

"Six and a half," Marie corrected, setting down mango juice. "I counted. And you chased a rabbit instead of finishing row seven."

Orange stared at her. "How did you — were you watching the whole time?"

"I'm your mother," Marie said, like that explained everything. "I see the rows. I see the rabbit. I see the blisters you're hiding. You push like you think the pain will leave on its own."

Orange looked down at his bowl. "...The rabbit started it."

"Finish your soup," Marie said, "and then you can go declare war on the rest of the field."

Outside, the village was fully awake now — and already loud.

Amy's voice carried clear across the lane, arguing with her father about whose catch had been bigger yesterday. "Mine!" "Yours was a frog, Amy." "It was a BIG frog!"

Leo hauled a basket of barn feed for Ben's barn past the smithy, knees wobbling, pretending it wasn't heavy. It was very heavy. His face said otherwise. His arms said the truth.

Lyra slipped between houses without a sound, bow over her shoulder, pausing just long enough to wave at Orange through the doorway before vanishing toward the forest edge.

Same morning. Same village. Same people Orange had known his whole life.

And yet —

Orange finished his juice in one happy gulp, wiped his mouth, and grabbed his hoe again. His palms still tingled where the blisters had been. His legs still hummed from the early rows. But under the tiredness, that warmth moved quietly, loosening what was tight, steadying what was shaky.

Not enough to make work easy. Just enough to let him keep going.

*What else can I do?* he thought, remembering the sprout in the dream leaning toward him. *If I work harder — if I learn how this really works — what happens?*

He didn't know. That was the annoying part. That was the exciting part.

He stepped back out into the morning light, hoe over his shoulder, bare feet in the cool dirt.

The field waited. The sun climbed. The mango leaves rustled like they were laughing at him.

"Okay!" Orange shouted to the rows, to the rabbit hiding somewhere, to the dream still warm behind his eyes. "Row seven! Then row eight! Then — we'll see!"

For the first time in his life, the field didn't feel like the boundary of his world.

It felt like the beginning of it.
